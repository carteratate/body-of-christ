# Rule 1 authorship verification: disputed patristic texts and persons

Research date: 28 September 2026

This memo checks the scholarly and ecclesiastical claims that decide which patristic texts stay in the TheoCorpus church-fathers collection. Each answer names the source that owns the claim. Where only a secondary summary was reachable, the entry says so and lowers its confidence.

Sources that could not be opened are listed at the end. Three kinds of source were blocked or paywalled: the Clavis Patrum Graecorum itself (Brepols login), the Sources Chrétiennes and De Gruyter sites (HTTP 403), and Cambridge Core reviews (HTTP 403). CPG numbers below were confirmed through the University of Fribourg's *Bibliothek der Kirchenväter* (BKV) work index, which keys every work to its CPG number and CPG Latin title. That confirms the number and title. It does not show the CPG's own notes on authorship.

Confidence levels:

- **High**: read in the primary source, or in a standard reference that reports the primary directly.
- **Medium**: read in a reliable secondary source (reference work, review, scholarly article), but not checked against the primary.
- **Low**: inferred, or found only in a secondary summary of a secondary source.

## 1. Apostolic Constitutions (CPG 1730)

### Date and place

**Answer:** Compiled in Syria, most likely Antioch, around 375-380. The range scholars give runs from about 350 to the early fifth century.

- The Catholic Encyclopedia puts the most probable date at "about the year 380," allows two decades either way, and locates it in Syria, probably Antioch. [J. B. Peterson, "Apostolic Constitutions," *Catholic Encyclopedia* 1 (1907)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01636a.htm)
- A teaching page that summarizes a standard reference gives "c.350-80" and "almost certainly of Syrian provenance." [L. Dysinger, "Apostolic Constitutions"](http://www.ldysinger.com/@texts/0388_apost_const/01_ap-con-sel.htm). The wording matches the *Oxford Dictionary of the Christian Church* entry, but I could not open the ODCC to confirm.
- The Sources Chrétiennes catalogue description of Metzger's edition (seen only as a search-result excerpt; the page returned 403) places the compiler in the East, perhaps at Antioch, toward the end of the fourth century.

**Confidence:** high for Syria/Antioch and the late fourth century. Medium for "375-380" as the exact window.

### The compiler's theology: consensus, majority, or contested?

**Answer:** All the sources agree that the compiler's theology is not Nicene. The Trullan council, Photius, and every modern editor say so. Most scholars since Turner (1914) and Hagedorn (1973) call him Arian. Whether he was a Neo-Arian (Eunomian), a homoian, or a moderate subordinationist is still contested. "Arian in some form" is the majority view. "Eunomian" is one argued position, not the consensus.

The sources, in order:

- **Photius, *Bibliotheca* cod. 112-113** (9th century). He finds the Constitutions open to censure on three counts: "clumsy fiction," "abusive charges against Deuteronomy," and "its Arianism, which can be refuted by a vigorous attack." He calls the companion Clementine *Recognitions* full of "blasphemy against the Son in accordance with the Arian heresy." [Photius, *Bibliotheca*, cod. 112-113, trans. J. H. Freese (1920)](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/photius_03bibliotheca.htm); Greek and French at [Remacle, *Bibliothèque de Photius* 112-113](https://remacle.org/bloodwolf/erudits/photius/clement3.htm) (Greek Ἀρειανισμῷ).
- **F. X. Funk** (*Die Apostolischen Konstitutionen*, 1891; edition *Didascalia et Constitutiones Apostolorum*, 1905). He defended the compiler against the charge of Arianism and called him an Apollinarian, because the compiler denies Christ a human soul. The Catholic Encyclopedia reports this ("as Dr. Funk would infer"), and so does Lightfoot on the parallel Ignatian question (see §2). [CE 1907](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01636a.htm)
- **C. H. Turner**, "Notes on the Apostolic Constitutions I. The Compiler an Arian," *JTS* 16 (Oct. 1914) 54-61. Turner argued that Funk's text hid the Arianism because it followed the wrong manuscript. In the third note of the series he looked back on the first. In his words, he had "pressed the conclusion that the author of the Constitutions was much more definitely Arian than the printed texts might have led us to suppose." [C. H. Turner, "Notes on the Apostolic Constitutions III," *JTS* 21 (1920) 160](https://biblicalstudies.org.uk/pdf/jts/021_160.pdf), p. 160. The same note (Apostolic Canon 1) holds that the Apostolic Canons and book 8 had a "common author."
- **D. Hagedorn**, *Der Hiobkommentar des Arianers Julian* (PTS 14, Berlin 1973). He identified the Arian Job commentator Julian with the compiler of the Constitutions and with the Ignatian interpolator. A contemporary review sums up the thesis: "ce mystérieux arien Julien n'est autre que le responsable" of both. [M. Aubineau, review, *Scriptorium* 30 (1976) 318](https://www.persee.fr/doc/scrip_0036-9772_1976_num_30_2_2810_t1_0318_0000_2). The review calls Julian "arien." Hagedorn's own front matter was blocked (403), so I could not confirm whether he labels Julian a Neo-Arian specifically.
- **M. Metzger**, *Les Constitutions apostoliques*, SC 320, 329, 336 (1985-1987). This is now the standard critical edition. A review confirms it replaced Funk's text. [A. Le Boulluec, *REG* 101 (1988) 210-211](https://www.persee.fr/doc/reg_0035-2039_1988_num_101_480_1535_t1_0210_0000_3). The SC catalogue text (search excerpt only) says Trullo judged the work contaminated because some formulas carry pre-Nicene subordinationism. I could not read Metzger's introduction to learn how he weighs the Arian and Eunomian readings. **Not verified.**
- **Later work.** T. A. Kopecek, "Neo-Arian Religion: The Evidence of the Apostolic Constitutions," in R. C. Gregg (ed.), *Arianism: Historical and Theological Reassessments* (1985) 153-179, argues for Neo-Arian theology. J. G. Mueller, "The Trinitarian Doctrine of the Apostolic Constitutions," *Studia Patristica* 92 (2017) 25-39, argues that Euzoius best represents it. B. Daley (2010) sees the influence of Meletius. A. Stewart (2025) finds the text "neither strongly Nicene … nor clearly Eunomian." These citations come from a scholar's blog used only to find sources: [A. Stewart, "Ps-Ignatius and the Apostolic Constitutions again" (2025)](https://ancientchurchorders.wordpress.com/2025/06/14/ps-ignatius-and-the-apostolic-constitutions-again/). I did not open Kopecek, Mueller, or Daley themselves.

**Confidence:** high that the text is heterodox by Nicene standards and that Arian readings dominate. Medium that the exact label is contested; the evidence is a spread of published positions, read through their citations.

### CPG 1730 on authorship

**Answer:** CPG 1730 is *Constitutiones Apostolorum*, filed under church orders (BKV: "Kirchenordnungen"), not under a named author. [BKV, CPG 1730](https://bkv.unifr.ch/de/works/cpg-1730/versions/apostolische-konstitutionen). I could not read the CPG entry's own notes on authorship.

**Confidence:** high for the number and the anonymous classification. Unverified for anything the CPG entry says beyond that.

## 2. Long (interpolated) recension of Ignatius

### Date

**Answer:** Latter half of the fourth century, from Syria (Antioch region). Funk once pushed it into the early fifth century, and Lightfoot rejected that.

- Lightfoot: "All the indications … point to the latter half of the fourth century," with a "general convergence of opinion" on that date. He answers Funk's later fifth-century dating, and he takes the writer's interest in Antioch, Laodicea, Tarsus and Anazarbus to point to Syria. [J. B. Lightfoot, *The Apostolic Fathers* II.1, 2nd ed. (1889) pp. 273-274](https://archive.org/details/apostolicfathep2v1clemuoft)
- The Catholic Encyclopedia dates the collection to "the latter part of the fourth century." It then says the interpolation was "extremely probably" the work of an Apollinarist writing "towards the beginning of the fifth century." [J. B. O'Connor, "St. Ignatius of Antioch," *CE* 7 (1910)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07644a.htm). These two statements pull against each other. The fifth-century date is Funk's.

**Confidence:** high.

### Is the interpolator the Apostolic Constitutions compiler?

**Answer:** This is a majority view, not a consensus. Ussher proposed it in the seventeenth century. Harnack (1884), Funk, Turner and Hagedorn held it. Lightfoot rejected it, and some recent scholars doubt it.

- Lightfoot rejects the identity in so many words. Harnack "supposes that the interpolator of the Ignatian letters was himself the interpolator of the Apostolic Constitutions. To this view I see serious objections." He argues that pseudo-Ignatius copied the Constitutions and sometimes misread them (the ages of Josiah and Obed-Edom), and that the two writers differ on the Roman succession. He also notes that Ussher's solution "has commonly been rejected" by later writers on the Constitutions. [Lightfoot, AF II.1 (1889) pp. 262-266 and p. 265 n. 2](https://archive.org/details/apostolicfathep2v1clemuoft)
- The CE says Funk makes the identification. Its article on the Constitutions calls the identity "now generally admitted" and traces it to Ussher. [CE Ignatius](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07644a.htm); [CE Apostolic Constitutions](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01636a.htm)
- Hagedorn (1973) makes one author, Julian, responsible for both. See §1.
- Stewart (2025) holds that the two are "likely not identical," though they are closely related. [Stewart blog, as above](https://ancientchurchorders.wordpress.com/2025/06/14/ps-ignatius-and-the-apostolic-constitutions-again/)

**Confidence:** high that the question is contested. The pattern (majority for identity, Lightfoot and some recent scholars against) is medium.

### The pseudo-Ignatius's own theology

Lightfoot finds it hard "to convict him of Arianism." He leans toward Arianism "without definitely crossing the border." The one clear heresy is that the writer denies Christ a human soul (*Philipp.* 5, *Philad.* 6), which is close to Apollinarianism but "not strictly so." [Lightfoot, AF II.1 pp. 267-273](https://archive.org/details/apostolicfathep2v1clemuoft). Stewart (2025) reads him as a traditional homoian.

**Confidence:** high for what Lightfoot says.

### The six spurious letters

**Answer:** The claim is correct. The six are: Mary of Cassobola to Ignatius, Ignatius to Mary at Neapolis (near Zarbus/Anazarbus), to the Tarsians, to the Philippians, to the Antiochians, and to Hero, a deacon of Antioch. "Mary of Cassobola" and "Mary at Neapolis" are the two halves of one exchange: her letter and his reply. New Advent's ANF text titles them "The Epistle of Maria the Proselyte to Ignatius" and "Epistle to Mary at Neapolis, Near Zarbus." [ANF 1, Spurious Epistles](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0114.htm); [CE Ignatius](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07644a.htm). CPG gives the long recension and these letters one number: **CPG 1026**, *Epistulae interpolatae et epistulae suppositiciae*. [BKV CPG 1026](https://bkv.unifr.ch/works/cpg-1026/versions/other-epistles-of-ignatius-2)

**Confidence:** high.

### Ussher's and Lightfoot's roles

- **Ussher** published the Latin version of the genuine middle recension in 1644 from two English manuscripts [CE Ignatius](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07644a.htm). He also first proposed that the Ignatian interpolator and the Constitutions compiler were the same person, or at least "of the same school" [Lightfoot AF II.1 pp. 262-263](https://archive.org/details/apostolicfathep2v1clemuoft). The CE article on Ignatius says Isaac Vossius published the Greek middle recension in 1646. I did not check that date against a primary source.
- **Lightfoot** wrote the standard defence of the seven-letter middle recension. He dated the long recension and the six added letters to the latter half of the fourth century and showed the Curetonian Syriac to be an abridgment (see below). [AF II.1 (1889)](https://archive.org/details/apostolicfathep2v1clemuoft)

**Confidence:** high.

### The four medieval Latin letters

**Answer:** They are spurious and medieval. There are two letters from Ignatius to St John, one from Ignatius to the Virgin, and the Virgin's reply. CPG lists them as **CPG 1028**, *Epistulae iv mediaevales (latine)*. [BKV CPG 1028](https://bkv.unifr.ch/works/cpg-1028/versions/other-epistles-of-ignatius). Lightfoot says they "are found only in Latin," which was probably their original language. He ties them to the Marian devotion of "the eleventh and following centuries" and calls the workmanship "coarse and clumsy." He also finds that the supposed endorsement by St Bernard rests on a misreading. [Lightfoot AF II.1 pp. 234-236](https://archive.org/details/apostolicfathep2v1clemuoft). The CE calls them "unanimously admitted to be spurious." [CE Ignatius](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07644a.htm)

**Confidence:** high.

### The Syriac short recension (Cureton)

**Answer:** It is an abridgment, not the original form. Cureton published it in 1845 and defended it as the only genuine text in *Vindiciae Ignatianae* (1846). Lightfoot, who once accepted Cureton's priority, came to call it "the Curetonian abridgment," as distinct from the original Syriac version. He treats it at length in *AF* II.1, pp. 280-327. [Lightfoot AF II.1, preface p. viii and pp. 280-327](https://archive.org/details/apostolicfathep2v1clemuoft). The CE: "It is now generally admitted that Cureton's Syriac version is only an abbreviation." [CE Ignatius](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07644a.htm)

**Confidence:** high.

## 3. Pseudo-Justin writings and Marcellus of Ancyra

### CPG numbers

| Work | CPG | Source |
|---|---|---|
| *De resurrectione* (fragments) | 1081 | [BKV](https://bkv.unifr.ch/en/works/cpg-1081) |
| *Oratio ad Graecos* | 1082 | [BKV](https://bkv.unifr.ch/en/works/cpg-1082); also W. Kinzig, [Tübingen repository article](https://tobias-lib.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/bitstream/handle/10900/123856/Kinzig_048.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y) |

| *De monarchia* ("On the Sole Government of God") | 1084 | [BKV](https://bkv.unifr.ch/en/works/cpg-1084) |

**Confidence:** high for the numbers.

### *Cohortatio ad Graecos*: authorship

**Answer:** Not by Justin. The main modern proposal is Marcellus of Ancyra: C. Riedweg, *Ps.-Justin (Markell von Ankyra?), Ad Graecos de vera religione (bisher "Cohortatio ad Graecos")*, 2 vols., Schweizerische Beiträge zur Altertumswissenschaft 25 (Basel 1994). The question mark is in Riedweg's own title. Other proposals:

- An objection by P. F. Beatrice drew a reply from B. Pouderon, "La Cohortatio du Pseudo-Justin doit-elle bien être attribuée à Marcel d'Ancyre ?" (bibliographic record only; ResearchGate returned 403).
- R. M. Grant (1958) and E. Digeser (2015 lecture) favour a pre-Constantinian date. [Oxford Patristics conference summary](http://oxfordpatristics.blogspot.com/2015/05/elizabeth-digeser-pseudo-justins.html), used only as a pointer.
- Older proposals included Apollinaris of Laodicea and Apollinaris of Hierapolis. I found these only in secondary summaries.
- The CE (1910) says the Justinian authenticity "has been defended without success" and reports Harnack's date of 180-240. [J. Lebreton, "St. Justin Martyr," CE 8 (1910)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08580c.htm)

I could not open Riedweg or the reviews of it (Cambridge 403; Persée showed only the first page of J. Schamp, *L'Antiquité Classique* 66 (1997) 470-472). So I cannot confirm how widely the Marcellus thesis is now accepted.

**Confidence:** high that it is not Justin's and that Riedweg proposed Marcellus. Medium that Marcellus is the leading hypothesis. Low on how many scholars accept it.

### *De monarchia*, *Oratio ad Graecos*, *De resurrectione*

- **De monarchia (1084)**: not by Justin; date uncertain. The CE calls it a "tract of uncertain date." Its forged Greek poetic quotations look like the work of an earlier Jewish or Christian anthology. [CE Justin](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08580c.htm) **Medium.**
- **Oratio ad Graecos (1082)**: not by Justin. The CE treats it as spurious and gives Harnack's range of 180-240. [CE Justin](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08580c.htm) **Medium.**
- **De resurrectione (1081)**: disputed. The CE (1910) thought the attribution to Justin "probable." M. Heimgartner, *Pseudojustin: Über die Auferstehung* (PTS 54, Berlin 2001), concludes that the treatise probably comes from the apologist Athenagoras. That summary comes from the publisher's abstract as quoted in search results; the De Gruyter page itself was blocked. **Medium** for the Athenagoras proposal. **Low** on how far it has been accepted.

### Was Marcellus condemned by name by a council the Catholic Church recognises?

**Answer:** No council recognized by the Catholic Church condemned him personally by name, as far as these sources show. The claim is correct as stated.

- **Constantinople I (381), canon 1** anathematizes "that of the Marcellians," that is, the party, alongside the Photinians and Apollinarians. It does not name Marcellus. [NPNF2 14, Constantinople I, canon 1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3808.htm)
- **Pope Julius I** accepted Marcellus's confession. His letter to the Eusebians says Marcellus "maintains nothing outside the truth" and reports testimony "to his orthodoxy." [Athanasius, *Apologia contra Arianos* 32](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/28081.htm)
- **The Council of Serdica** (343; Western bishops) read his book and "his faith was found to be correct." It pronounced him, with Athanasius and Asclepas, "innocent and clear of offense." [*Apol. c. Ar.* 47, and the synodal letter that follows](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/28081.htm)
- The CE says Athanasius, Damasus (380) and the Second Council later "pronounced against him." [J. Arendzen, "Marcellus of Ancyra," CE 9 (1910)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09642a.htm). This is the CE's reading. The council text names only the Marcellians.
- The heresiarch list in the pseudo-Gelasian decree names Photinus but not Marcellus. [Decretum Gelasianum, ed. von Dobschütz](https://www.tertullian.org/decretum.htm)

**Confidence:** high for the texts of canon 1, Julius, and Serdica. Medium for the negative claim that no recognized council condemned him by name.

## 4. Gregory Thaumaturgus: disputed works

### "Sectional Confession of Faith" (*Kata meros pistis*)

**Answer:** Its Apollinarian authorship is established. The case goes back to the sixth century and was proved in modern times by C. P. Caspari (1879) and H. Lietzmann (1904). CPG files it under Apollinaris as **CPG 3645**. [P. Gemeinhardt, "Apollinaris of Laodicea," *ZAC* 10 (2006) n. 18](https://www.db-thueringen.de/servlets/MCRFileNodeServlet/dbt_derivate_00058058/10.1515_ZAC.2006.021.pdf). BKV lists the ANF translation under Gregory with no CPG number ("x").

- **Lietzmann**, *Apollinaris von Laodicea und seine Schule. Texte und Untersuchungen* (Tübingen 1904), p. 129. He says two independent witnesses make the work Apollinaris's: Theodoret, and the author of *Adversus fraudes*. There is "kein vernünftiger grund" to doubt it. Anyone wanting the full proof should read "Caspari's sorgfältige untersuchung," *Alte und neue Quellen* (1879) pp. 65 ff. Lietzmann edits the text at pp. 167-185. [Lietzmann 1904, archive.org](https://archive.org/details/apollinarisvonl01apolgoog)
- **Adversus fraudes Apollinistarum** names it. The opening complains that Apollinarists put the names of Gregory Thaumaturgus, Athanasius and Julius on Apollinaris's works, so that one finds among the orthodox "τὴν κατὰ μέρος Ἀπολλιναρίου πίστιν Γρηγορίου ἐπιγεγραμμένην." Its florilegium, items 39-40, then quotes the KMP as Apollinaris's own. Printed in PG 86/2, 1947-1976, from Mai, *Spicilegium Romanum* X.2, 128-151. [Lietzmann pp. 103-106](https://archive.org/details/apollinarisvonl01apolgoog)
- **Who wrote *Adversus fraudes*** is itself uncertain. Loofs dated it to the early sixth century (before about 520). Lietzmann says the attribution to Leontius of Byzantium remains undecided and that the date is enough for his purposes. [Lietzmann p. 106](https://archive.org/details/apollinarisvonl01apolgoog). The CE calls it an "anonymous book." [CE, "Apollinarianism"](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01615b.htm)
- Lietzmann also notes that the *Adversus fraudes* text is a reworked edition that looks like a sharpened version by a pupil. Gemeinhardt (2006) likewise says the KMP "has been revised by Apollinaris' pupils."

**Confidence:** high.

## 5. Council in Trullo (692), canon 2

### Wording on the Apostolic Canons and the Constitutions

The NPNF translation: the eighty-five canons, "received and ratified by the holy and blessed Fathers before us, and also handed down to us in the name of the holy and glorious Apostles," are to "remain firm and unshaken." The canon admits that these canons bid the faithful receive the Constitutions "[written] by Clement." It then says heretics had introduced "adulterous matter … clean contrary to piety," and concludes: "We therefore reject these Constitutions." [NPNF2 14, Council in Trullo, canon 2](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3814.htm)

**Confidence:** high.

### Did the Latin Church receive Trullo?

**Answer:** Not as such, and never formally.

- **Sergius I** refused to sign. The *Liber Pontificalis* says he would not consent because some chapters were "extra ritum ecclesiasticum," choosing "ante mori quam novitatum erroribus consentire." [PBE, Sergios 30, citing *LP* 86.6-9 and Paul the Deacon, *Hist. Lang.* 6.11](https://pbe.kcl.ac.uk/data/D67/F87.htm) **High.**
- **John VII** declined to countersign. **Constantine I** reached an oral compromise with Justinian II. **Hadrian I** wrote to Tarasius that he received the sixth council "with all its canons." [G. Nedungatt, "The Council in Trullo Revisited," *Theological Studies* 71 (2010) 651-676, at pp. 669-671](https://theologicalstudies.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/10.1177.004056391007100307.pdf). NPNF quotes Hadrian's letter. **Medium.**
- **John VIII** accepted "all those canons which did not contradict the true faith, good morals, and the decrees of Rome." [NPNF2 14, Trullo, Introductory Note](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nicene_and_Post-Nicene_Fathers:_Series_II/Volume_XIV/Additional_Canons_2/Introductory_Note). The underlying report is Anastasius Bibliothecarius's preface to his Latin translation of Nicaea II, which I did not open. **Medium.**
- **Gratian** took 16 Trullan canons into the *Decretum* as canons of the sixth synod. [Nedungatt pp. 669-671](https://theologicalstudies.net/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/10.1177.004056391007100307.pdf) **Medium.**
- **Hefele**, as quoted in NPNF: Nicaea II ascribed the Trullan canons to the Sixth Council, but "their own canons have never received the ratification of the Holy See." [NPNF intro](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nicene_and_Post-Nicene_Fathers:_Series_II/Volume_XIV/Additional_Canons_2/Introductory_Note)

### Nicaea II (787), canon 1

**Answer:** Canon 1 confirms the canons "set forth by the holy trumpets of the Spirit, the renowned Apostles, or by the Six Ecumenical Councils, or by Councils locally assembled … or by our holy Fathers." It does not name Trullo. Because the Greeks counted the Trullan canons as the Sixth Council's, it confirms them by that route. [NPNF2 14, Nicaea II, canon 1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3819.htm)

**How the Catholic Church regards this now:** John Paul II's apostolic constitution *Sacri canones* (18 October 1990), which promulgated the Eastern Code, opens by citing Nicaea II canon 1, adopted under the presidency of Hadrian I's legates. It says the council confirmed the sacred canons as one body of law, a "Codex" for all the Eastern Churches. It adds that the Quinisext Synod in Trullo (dated there to 691) had already done so, "in secundo suo canone distinctius circumscribendo." [*Sacri canones*, Latin text, vatican.va](https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/la/apost_constitutions/documents/hf_jp-ii_apc_19901018_sacri-canones.html). So the Holy See treats Trullo's canon 2 corpus as the canonical patrimony of the Eastern Churches, not as universal Latin law. Nedungatt notes that Latin lists of ecumenical councils rest on custom, not on a magisterial definition (pp. 657-658).

**Confidence:** high for the texts. Medium for the summary of the Catholic position.

## 6. Apostolic Canons in the West

**Answer:** The claim is correct, with refinements.

- **Dionysius Exiguus** translated the first 50 canons into Latin around 500 and left out 51-85. He dropped them altogether from a later edition made under Hormisdas. Even in the first edition he admitted that many in the West hesitated to accept them. [T. Shahan, "Apostolic Canons," CE 3 (1908)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/03279a.htm) **High** for the 50. **Medium** for the Hormisdas edition detail.
- **Western reception.** The Latin Middle Ages recognized only the fifty. Gratian (about 1140) put excerpts into the *Decretum*, which gave them general use. [CE 3](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/03279a.htm) **Medium** (not checked against Gratian's text).
- **Gelasian Decree.** The whole book, not just canons 51-85, is listed as apocryphal: "Liber qui appellatur Canones apostolorum apocryphus." [Decretum Gelasianum, Latin, ed. von Dobschütz, TU 38.4 (1912)](https://www.tertullian.org/decretum.htm). The CE thinks this entry was "probably not in the original." The claim that "the rest" was listed as apocryphal is slightly off: the list names the book as a whole.
- **Status of the decree.** It is not a genuine act of Gelasius. Von Dobschütz (1912) concluded it is a pseudonymous private compilation of the first half of the sixth century, between 519 and 553, from Italy. [F. C. Burkitt, review of von Dobschütz, *JTS* 14 (1913) 469-471](https://www.tertullian.org/articles/burkitt_gelasianum.htm). The CE's 1909 article on Gelasius still treats the catalogue as his. [CE, "Pope St. Gelasius I"](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06406a.htm). **High** that modern scholarship holds this.
- **Common author.** Turner (1920) and the CE hold that the Apostolic Canons come from the same Syrian hand as the Constitutions. [Turner, *JTS* 21 (1920), note III on Apostolic Canon 1](https://biblicalstudies.org.uk/pdf/jts/021_160.pdf)

## 7. Rule 2 persons

### (a) Origen

**Answer:** Constantinople II (553), anathema 11, names him: "Arius, Eunomius, Macedonius, Apollinaris, Nestorius, Eutyches and Origen." [NPNF2 14, Constantinople II, canon 11](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3812.htm)

- **Interpolation debate.** The CE on the council notes that the acts were tampered with (as found in 680) and may be incomplete. It reports that Hefele, "with Cardinal Noris," held that Origen was condemned "only en passant" and that his name in anathema 11 "is not an interpolation." [T. Shahan, "Second Council of Constantinople," CE 4 (1908)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04308b.htm) **High** that the debate exists and for Hefele's position.
- **The fifteen anathemas.** The CE on Origen says the bishops "certainly subscribed" to fifteen anathemas proposed by the emperor before the council formally opened. It calls this an "extra-conciliary sentence," later "mistaken" for a decree of the council. It adds that Popes Vigilius, Pelagius I, Pelagius II and Gregory the Great speak of the council only in terms of the Three Chapters. [F. Prat, "Origen and Origenism," CE 11 (1911)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/11306b.htm) **High** for the CE's position.
- **Pseudo-Gelasian Decree.** It accepts "some works of Origen, which the blessed man Jerome does not reject" and refuses "the rest with their author." [Decretum, English](https://www.tertullian.org/decretum_eng.htm)
- **Benedict XVI.** The general audiences present him without censure. 25 April 2007 calls him "a true 'maestro'" and "an exemplary witness." 2 May 2007 speaks of his "primordial role" in *lectio divina*. Neither mentions a condemnation. [25 April 2007](https://www.vatican.va/content/benedict-xvi/en/audiences/2007/documents/hf_ben-xvi_aud_20070425.html); [2 May 2007](https://www.vatican.va/content/benedict-xvi/en/audiences/2007/documents/hf_ben-xvi_aud_20070502.html) **High.**

### (b) Tertullian

**Answer:** He separated from the Church in his last period. Benedict XVI's audience of 30 May 2007 says his rigorism "gradually led him away from communion with the Church to belong to the Montanist sect." It adds that he nonetheless never forgot that the Church is the Mother of our faith. [30 May 2007](https://www.vatican.va/content/benedict-xvi/en/audiences/2007/documents/hf_ben-xvi_aud_20070530.html). The pseudo-Gelasian Decree lists "opuscula Tertulliani" among the apocrypha. **High.**

### (c) Novatian

**Answer:** The claim is correct. Eusebius reports a Roman synod of sixty bishops under Cornelius which decreed that "Novatus" and his followers "should be considered by the church as strangers." [Eusebius, *HE* 6.43](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250106.htm). Eusebius spells the name "Novatus," which is the Greek form for Novatian. **High.** The date 251 is standard but is not stated in the passage.

### (d) Tatian and the Encratites

**Answer:** The claim needs a correction. Irenaeus does **not** make Tatian the founder of the Encratites. *Adv. Haer.* 1.28.1 says the Encratites sprang "from Saturninus and Marcion." Tatian, a hearer of Justin, left the Church after Justin's martyrdom and "first introduced the blasphemy" that Adam is not saved, "an opinion due entirely to himself." [Irenaeus, *Adv. Haer.* 1.28.1](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103128.htm). Eusebius is the one who makes him the author: "It is said that Tatian was the author of this false doctrine." [Eusebius, *HE* 4.28-29](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/250104.htm). Epiphanius (*Panarion* 46-47) and later writers follow this line; I did not check them. **High.**

### (e) Alexander of Lycopolis

**Answer:** Current scholarship makes him a pagan Platonist, not a Christian bishop.

- J. van Oort, "The Platonist Philosopher Alexander of Lycopolis on Manichaeism," *Journal of Early Christian History* 2.1 (2012) 86-94. Alexander was "neither converted to Manichaeism, nor was he a Christian bishop." The abstract says he is "now generally regarded as a pagan philosopher." [University of Pretoria repository](https://repository.up.ac.za/handle/2263/21160). The "2019" date in the question is the reprint in van Oort's Brill collection of essays on Mani and Augustine.
- Van Oort traces the bishop legend to Photius and to a mistranslation of the manuscript title by the first editor, Combefis. He calls the phrase "converted from the Gentiles" "a pious invention."
- **Where Photius says it.** Van Oort puts the reference in the *Bibliotheca*. The CE and the ANF introduction put it in Photius's *Contra Manichaeos* 1.11, which makes Alexander "Archbishop of Lycopolis." [CE, "Alexander of Lycopolis"](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01299a.htm) (seen via search excerpt). I did not open Photius to settle which work it is.

**Confidence:** high for the current view. Medium for the exact Photius locus.

### (f) Hippolytus

**Answer:** He is a reconciled schismatic venerated as a saint. The claim is correct, with one source correction.

- The primary witnesses are in the **Chronograph of 354**, not the *Liber Pontificalis* proper, though the Liberian Catalogue is the base of the LP. The Liberian Catalogue under Pontian: "Pontianus episcopus et Yppolitus presbiter exoles sunt deportati in Sardinia," in the consulship of Severus and Quintianus (235). [Chronograph of 354, bishops of Rome](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/chronography_of_354_13_bishops_of_rome.htm). The *Depositio martyrum*: "idus Aug. Ypoliti in Tiburtina et Pontiani in Callisti." [Chronograph of 354, depositions of martyrs](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/chronography_of_354_12_depositions_martyrs.htm) **High.**
- The CE says he set himself up as a rival bishop to Callistus. It also says Damasus's epitaph makes him a presbyter of the Novatian schism who urged his followers to reconcile before his death. [J. P. Kirsch, "St. Hippolytus of Rome," CE 7 (1910)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07360c.htm) **Medium** (I did not open the Damasus epigram).
- The General Roman Calendar keeps Saints Pontian and Hippolytus on 13 August as an optional memorial. I confirmed this only through [Catholic Culture's calendar](https://www.catholicculture.org/culture/liturgicalyear/calendar/day.cfm?date=2018-08-13), not vatican.va. **Medium.**

### Refutation of All Heresies (CPG 1899)

**Answer:** CPG lists it under Hippolytus as 1899, *Refutatio omnium haeresium*. [BKV CPG 1899](https://bkv.unifr.ch/en/works/cpg-1899). Authorship is contested. The manuscripts ascribe it to Origen, which is impossible. The attribution to Hippolytus is modern. The CE (1910) says most scholars then held Hippolytus "undoubtedly its author." A current reviewer states that the attribution "is modern, but mistaken" and describes the author as the unnamed leader of a Roman schismatic group, writing in the 220s. [P. W. van der Horst, review of M. D. Litwa, *Refutation of All Heresies* (2016), *BMCR* 2016.12.05](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2016/2016.12.05/). I could not see whether the CPG entry flags the dispute.

**Confidence:** high that it is disputed. Unverified for the CPG's own wording.

## 8. Other survivors a critic may question

### The pseudo-Gelasian "apocrypha" list

The decree lists these among the apocrypha: "opuscula Commodiani," "opuscula alterius Clementis Alexandrini," "opuscula Arnobii," "opuscula Lactantii sive Firmiani," "opuscula Victorini Petabionensis," and "opuscula Tertulliani." It also lists the works of Cassian and Tyconius, of a Thascius Cyprianus, and Eusebius's *History* (which the decree elsewhere says may still be read). [Decretum Gelasianum, Latin](https://www.tertullian.org/decretum.htm); [English](https://www.tertullian.org/decretum_eng.htm). The inclusion of John Cassian, a saint, shows that "apocrypha" in this list means "not for public or authoritative use," not "condemned heresy." The decree is itself a sixth-century private compilation (see §6). **High.**

### Conciliar or papal condemnations of the persons

I found no conciliar or papal condemnation by name of Commodian, Arnobius or Lactantius in the reference works consulted. The CE on Commodian cites only "the condemnation of Pseudo-Gelasius" and calls his theology unreliable (millenarian, apparently patripassian). [CE, "Commodianus"](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04165a.htm). The CE on Lactantius records no condemnation. [CE, "Lactantius"](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08736a.htm). **Medium.** This is a negative result from a limited set of sources, and I did not open the CE article on Arnobius.

### Victorinus of Pettau

He is a martyr-bishop listed in the Roman Martyrology on 2 November. The CE says his works were ranked with the apocrypha because of millenarianism. [CE, "St. Victorinus"](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15414a.htm). Jerome revised his Apocalypse commentary at the request of one Anatolius and rewrote the millenarian comments on Revelation 20-22. Haussleiter's edition (CSEL 49, 1916) prints both the original and Jerome's recension, with Jerome's letter to Anatolius at pp. 14 ff. [F. F. Bruce, "The Earliest Latin Commentary on the Apocalypse," *Evangelical Quarterly* 10 (1938) 352-366](https://biblicalstudies.org.uk/pdf/eq/1938-4_352.pdf) (seen via search excerpt). **Medium-high.** For retrieval, the two recensions should be labelled separately.

### Clement of Alexandria: the Martyrology claim is inaccurate

**Answer:** Benedict XIV did not *remove* Clement from the Roman Martyrology. He *declined to add* him. His apostolic letter *Postquam intelleximus* to King John V of Portugal is dated at Santa Maria Maggiore, 1 July 1748, and prefaces the 1749 Martyrology. In §§ XIX-XXXVIII it takes up the request to write Clement's name under 4 December. Benedict notes that Baronius had not carried the name over from Usuard's martyrology ("in Romanum Martyrologium non transtulit"). He gives three reasons against adding it: silence about Clement's life, no evidence of public cult, and works "sin minus erronea, saltem suspecta." He also discusses the Gelasian listing. [*Martyrologium Romanum* (1749), with Benedict XIV's letter, archive.org](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_pl0AUPENnqoC), front matter §§ XIX-XXVIII.

The CE says the name was "dropped" when the Martyrology was revised under Clement VIII on Baronius's advice. It says Benedict XIV "maintained this decision" for the same three reasons. [F. Havey, "Clement of Alexandria," CE 4 (1908)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04045a.htm). The CE and Benedict agree on the reasons. They differ on whether the name was ever in the Roman Martyrology. Benedict's own wording is the stronger source.

**Confidence:** high.

## 9. The Pfaff fragments of Irenaeus

**Answer:** The claim is correct. C. M. Pfaff published four Greek fragments in 1715, supposedly from a Turin manuscript. Funk showed them to be apocryphal. Harnack proved Pfaff forged them himself: A. Harnack, *Die Pfaff'schen Irenäus-Fragmente als Fälschungen Pfaffs nachgewiesen*, TU 20.3 (NF 5.3), Leipzig 1900, pp. 1-69. The CE: "Harnack has established the fact that Pfaff himself fabricated them." [A. Poncelet, "St. Irenaeus," CE 8 (1910)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08130b.htm); bibliographic record at [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Die_Pfaff_schen_Iren%C3%A4us_Fragmente_als_f.html?id=HCuY3MWcDR8C). **High.** Any Pfaff fragment in the corpus should be removed or labelled as an eighteenth-century forgery.

## 10. Other attributions

### Epistle to Diognetus, chapters 11-12 (CPG 1112)

**Answer:** Most editors treat them as a later addition by another hand. The CE: "florid and obscure," with "no relation to the rest," and they "seem to be a fragment of a homily of later date." The writer calls himself a "disciple of the Apostles," which later led to the letter being counted among the Apostolic Fathers. [J. Chapman, "Epistle to Diognetus," CE 5 (1909)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05008b.htm). The single manuscript burned at Strasbourg in 1870. I did not check modern defences of the letter's unity. **Medium-high** for "separate addition" as the majority view.

### Muratorian fragment

**Answer:** The author is unknown. Caius of Rome is one old conjecture among several: Papias, Hegesippus, Caius, Hippolytus, Rhodon, Melito. The CE says "no well founded hypothesis has been adduced." It dates the fragment to Rome around 180-200. [J. P. Kirsch, "Muratorian Canon," CE 10 (1911)](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/10642a.htm). The later argument for a fourth-century eastern date (Sundberg 1973, Hahneman 1992) is well known but was not checked here. **High** that the author is unknown. **Medium** on the date debate.

### Canons of Hippolytus (CPG 1742)

**Answer:** They are an Egyptian church order based on the *Apostolic Tradition*, dated about 336-340. CPG 1742 is *Canones Hippolyti*. [BKV CPG 1742](https://bkv.unifr.ch/en/works/cpg-1742). P. F. Bradshaw (ed.), *The Canons of Hippolytus*, trans. C. Bebawi (Alcuin/GROW Liturgical Study 2, Grove Books 1987), follows R.-G. Coquin's edition (*Patrologia Orientalis* 31.2, 1966) on date and place. I reached this only through catalogue and search summaries; the full text was not reachable. **Medium.**

### Acts of Archelaus = Hegemonius

**Answer:** The claim is correct. Photius reports that Heraclian of Chalcedon, in his work *Against the Manichaeans*, names "Hegemonius, who wrote out the disputation of Archelaus against Manes." [Photius, *Bibliotheca* cod. 85, trans. Freese](https://www.tertullian.org/fathers/photius_03bibliotheca.htm). **High** for the ancient attribution. The historicity of Archelaus himself and the fourth-century date are standard (see the Vermes and Lieu translation, 2001) but were not checked here.

## Claims in the brief that are wrong or need correcting

1. **Clement of Alexandria.** Benedict XIV did not remove him from the Roman Martyrology. Baronius never carried him over from Usuard, and Benedict's 1748 letter *Postquam intelleximus* declined to add him (§8).
2. **Tatian.** Irenaeus *Adv. Haer.* 1.28.1 does not name Tatian founder of the Encratites. He derives them from Saturninus and Marcion and credits Tatian with one doctrine. Eusebius *HE* 4.28-29 is the source for "author of the heresy" (§7d).
3. **Ignatian interpolator = AC compiler.** This is a majority view, not a general one. Lightfoot explicitly rejected it, against Harnack. Funk and Hagedorn affirmed it. Stewart (2025) doubts it (§2).
4. **Compiler's heterodoxy.** "Arian in some form" is the majority reading. "Eunomian" is one argued position (Kopecek, Mueller), not the consensus. Funk's label was Apollinarian (§1).
5. **Hippolytus sources.** The exile and deposition notices are in the Chronograph of 354 (Liberian Catalogue and *Depositio martyrum*), which the *Liber Pontificalis* later reused (§7f).
6. **Gelasian Decree.** It lists the whole book of the Apostolic Canons as apocryphal, not only canons 51-85. The decree itself is a sixth-century pseudonymous compilation, not a papal act (§6).
7. **Alexander of Lycopolis.** Van Oort's article is from 2012 (JECH), reprinted later. Photius's reference is placed in *Contra Manichaeos* 1.11 by the CE and ANF, and in the *Bibliotheca* by van Oort (§7e).
8. **Adversus fraudes.** It does name the *Kata meros pistis* as Apollinaris's. Its attribution to Leontius of Byzantium is itself uncertain (Lietzmann p. 106). Lietzmann's book is 1904 (§4).
9. **Marcellus.** Constantinople I canon 1 anathematizes the "Marcellians," not Marcellus by name. This matches the brief's own parenthesis (§3).
10. **Trullo's date.** *Sacri canones* dates it to 691. Most modern works give 691-692 or 692.
